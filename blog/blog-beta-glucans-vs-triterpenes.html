Beta-Glucans vs Triterpenes: The Two Active Fractions in Mushroom Extracts

By Natural Sleep Supplements Editorial Team | Published September 2026

Two words show up again and again on mushroom supplement labels: beta-glucans and triterpenes. Brands use them as shorthand for quality, but most shoppers never learn what they actually are or why a product might feature one and ignore the other. This explainer covers both compound families at a mechanism level, without any invented numbers, so you can read a label with a clearer eye.

This is educational content about how these compounds are categorized. It is not medical advice and does not claim that any supplement is a remedy for a health condition.

The Simple Version

A mushroom extract is a mixture of hundreds of compounds. Two families get most of the attention because they are abundant, measurable, and characteristic of the mushroom. Beta-glucans are the water-loving structural sugars. Triterpenes are the oil-loving bitter molecules. They live in different chemical neighborhoods, they are pulled out by different solvents, and they are associated with different traditional uses.

Beta-Glucans

Beta-glucans are polysaccharides, which simply means long chains of sugar molecules linked together. They are part of the structural material of the mushroom's cell walls. Because they dissolve in water, they are the fraction captured by hot water extraction and by traditional mushroom teas and broths.

Beta-glucans are the compound family most often discussed in the context of general immune and wellness support. They are also the fraction most supplement testing programs try to quantify, which is why you will sometimes see a product standardized to a beta-glucan percentage.

Triterpenes

Triterpenes are a completely different class of molecule. They are built from a six-unit isoprene backbone and are fat-soluble rather than water-soluble. In reishi specifically, the best-known triterpenes are the ganoderic acids, and they are responsible for the mushroom's famously bitter taste.

Triterpenes are the fraction associated with reishi's traditional reputation for calm. Because they do not dissolve well in water, a hot water extract alone tends to be low in triterpenes, which is a common blind spot in cheaper products.

Why the Difference Matters When You Shop

The practical consequence is that the two fractions do not travel together automatically. A product can be rich in beta-glucans and nearly empty of triterpenes, or the reverse, depending entirely on how it was made.

  • A hot water extract favors beta-glucans and tends to be low in triterpenes.
  • An alcohol extract favors triterpenes and tends to be low in beta-glucans.
  • A dual extract is the method designed to carry both fractions in one finished product.

So when a label brags about one fraction, the quiet question is what happened to the other. A reishi product that leads with beta-glucans and never mentions triterpenes may simply be a hot water extract, which is fine as long as you know that is what you are buying.

A Word on the Bitter Taste

Bitterness is a rough and imperfect real-world signal. Triterpenes such as ganoderic acids are intensely bitter, so a genuine full-spectrum reishi extract usually tastes bitter. A reishi product that tastes bland or sweet may be heavy on flavorings or light on the triterpene fraction. Taste is not a substitute for lab data, but a suspiciously pleasant reishi is worth a second look at the ingredient panel.

The honest limits of this framing: beta-glucans and triterpenes are the headline families, but they are not the whole story. A full extract also contains amino acids, sterols, and many minor compounds. Reducing a mushroom to two numbers is convenient for marketing and useful for shopping, but it is still a simplification of a complex natural material.

What About "Percentages" on the Label

Some products list a beta-glucan percentage, and a standardized figure is genuinely more informative than the vaguer "polysaccharides" claim, which can include starch from grain a mushroom was grown on. However, a percentage is only as trustworthy as the lab behind it. Look for the fraction to be verified by a third-party certificate of analysis rather than asserted on the front of the box. Triterpene content is harder to standardize and is less commonly reported, so its absence from a label is not automatically a red flag.

How to Use This When Reading a Product

  1. Identify which fraction the label is emphasizing.
  2. Ask what the extraction method implies about the other fraction.
  3. If both fractions matter to you, confirm the product is genuinely dual-extracted.
  4. Prefer standardized figures that are backed by third-party testing over front-of-box adjectives.
  5. Use taste as a loose sanity check for reishi, never as proof.

Key Takeaways

  • Beta-glucans are water-soluble structural sugars; triterpenes are fat-soluble bitter molecules.
  • The two fractions are captured by different extraction methods and do not automatically appear together.
  • A product that highlights one fraction may be quietly low in the other.
  • In reishi, ganoderic acids are the signature triterpenes, and they taste bitter.
  • Standardized figures are more useful than vague claims, but only when backed by third-party testing.

Once you can separate the two fractions in your head, mushroom labels stop being a wall of jargon. You can see what a product is optimized for, infer what it may be missing, and ask the one question that most marketing hopes you will skip: where is the other fraction.

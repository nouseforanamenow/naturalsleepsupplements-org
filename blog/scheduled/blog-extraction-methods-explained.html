Water, Alcohol, or Dual Extraction: A Buyer's Guide to Mushroom Extracts

By Natural Sleep Supplements Editorial Team | Published September 2026

If you have shopped for a reishi or lion's mane product, you have probably seen the words "dual-extracted" or "hot water extract" on the label and wondered whether they matter. They do. The extraction method decides which parts of the mushroom actually end up in the bottle, and two products made from the same raw mushroom can contain very different compounds depending on how they were processed.

This guide explains the three common methods in plain language, what each one pulls out, and how to use that information as a buyer. It is educational only and is not a recommendation to use any product for a medical purpose.

Why Extraction Exists at All

A raw mushroom is mostly indigestible fiber. The cell walls are built from chitin, the same tough material found in insect shells, and your digestive system cannot easily break it down. Many of the compounds people are interested in are locked inside those cell walls. Extraction is the step that breaks the walls open and dissolves the useful compounds into a liquid you can concentrate and standardize.

The catch is that different compounds dissolve in different solvents. Some are water-loving and some are not. That single fact is why the method matters so much.

The Three Common Methods

Hot Water Extraction

The mushroom is simmered in hot water for an extended period. This releases the water-soluble compounds, most notably the beta-glucans, a family of polysaccharides that give many mushroom extracts their reputation. Hot water is gentle, food-like, and alcohol-free, which is why it is the traditional method used in teas and broths for centuries.

Pulls out: beta-glucans and other polysaccharides, along with water-soluble amino acids and some smaller molecules.

Misses: the bitter, oil-loving compounds that do not dissolve in water.

Alcohol (Ethanol) Extraction

The mushroom sits in food-grade alcohol, which dissolves the compounds that water leaves behind. In reishi these include the triterpenes, the bitter molecules many people associate with the mushroom's calming reputation. Alcohol extraction is efficient at capturing these fat-soluble fractions, but on its own it does a poor job with beta-glucans.

Pulls out: triterpenes and other non-water-soluble compounds.

Misses: most of the beta-glucan fraction.

Dual Extraction

This is a two-step process that runs both a hot water extraction and an alcohol extraction, then recombines the two liquids into one finished extract. The goal is a product that carries both the water-soluble and the alcohol-soluble fractions, so you are not forced to choose between them.

Pulls out: both beta-glucans and triterpenes, giving the broadest compound profile of the three.

Trade-off: it is more labor-intensive and usually costs more to produce.

Alcohol-Free Does Not Mean Water-Only

Here is a point of confusion worth clearing up. A finished product can be alcohol-free even if alcohol was used during processing. In a well-made dual extract, the alcohol is a processing solvent that is largely removed or reduced during finishing, so the label may still read alcohol-free while the triterpene fraction remains. If avoiding alcohol in the final bottle matters to you, look for a clear statement on the label and, when available, a certificate of analysis that reports residual solvent levels.

How to Read the Method on a Label

Labels are inconsistent across the category, so a little translation helps.

  • "Hot water extract" or "polysaccharide standardized": the product is emphasizing the beta-glucan fraction. Reasonable for an alcohol-free preference, but it may be light on triterpenes.
  • "Alcohol extract" or "tincture": the product is emphasizing triterpenes and similar compounds. It may be light on beta-glucans.
  • "Dual-extracted" or "full-spectrum": the product is aiming to capture both fractions. This is the broadest profile, though the phrase full-spectrum is not regulated, so verify with testing when you can.
  • "Extract ratio" such as 8:1: this tells you how much raw material went into the concentrate, not which compounds are present. A high ratio and a poor method still leaves compounds behind.

Extract Versus Whole Mushroom Powder

Some inexpensive products are simply dried, milled mushroom or, more often, milled mycelium grown on grain. These are not extracts. Because the cell walls were never opened, much of the compound content stays locked inside the fiber and passes through undigested. A ground powder can be a fine food, but it is not the same thing as a concentrated extract, and it should not be priced or marketed as one.

Quick buyer heuristic: match the method to the compounds you care about. If you want the beta-glucan fraction, hot water is required. If you want the triterpene fraction, alcohol is required. If you want both in one bottle, dual extraction is the only method that delivers them together.

What the Method Cannot Tell You

Extraction method is a useful filter, but it is not a guarantee of quality. Two dual extracts can still differ widely based on the raw material, the growing conditions, the concentration, and the care taken during processing. Method tells you which categories of compounds are possible in the bottle. Third-party testing tells you what is actually there. Use the method as the first screen and lab verification as the confirmation.

Key Takeaways

  • Extraction breaks open tough mushroom cell walls so compounds become bioavailable.
  • Hot water captures beta-glucans; alcohol captures triterpenes; dual extraction captures both.
  • Alcohol-free in the final product is possible even when alcohol was used as a processing solvent.
  • Extract ratios describe concentration, not which compounds are present.
  • Milled powder is not an extract, because the cell walls were never opened.
  • Method narrows the possibilities; third-party testing confirms what is really in the bottle.

Understanding extraction turns a confusing shelf into a readable one. Once you can tell a hot water extract from an alcohol extract from a true dual extract, you can quickly judge whether a product's method fits what you are actually shopping for, and you can spot the products that are hoping you will not ask.

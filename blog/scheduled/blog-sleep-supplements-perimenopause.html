Sleep Supplements for Perimenopause: What Actually Helps

By Natural Sleep Supplements Editorial Team | Published September 2026

If you are searching for the best supplement for sleep during perimenopause, here is the honest short answer: the ingredients most people find worth trying target the calming and temperature-regulating side of sleep rather than hormones themselves. Magnesium (often as magnesium glycinate), glycine, L-theanine, and reishi are the four most commonly reached for, and each maps to a specific symptom. None of them replace a conversation with your clinician about what is driving your sleep changes.

Perimenopause disrupts sleep in its own way, so the supplement that helped a friend in her twenties may do little for you now. Understanding why the disruption happens makes it much easier to choose well.

Why Perimenopausal Sleep Is Different

During the years leading up to menopause, estrogen and progesterone rise and fall unpredictably. Two features of that shift matter most for sleep. First, progesterone has a naturally calming, GABA-related character, so as it dips, the nervous system can feel less settled at night. Second, changing estrogen influences the body's temperature control, which is why night sweats and warm, restless awakenings become common. The result is often a lighter, more fragmented night: falling asleep is not always the hard part, but staying asleep is.

That is the key insight for choosing a supplement. Match the ingredient to the symptom pattern you actually have, not to a generic "sleep aid" label.

Racing or anxious mind at night? Calming-pathway ingredients like L-theanine and magnesium glycinate are the usual starting point.

Warm, sweaty, temperature-driven awakenings? Glycine and a cool bedroom target thermoregulation.

Light, frequently interrupted sleep? Magnesium and glycine are the most commonly discussed options.

Ingredient by Ingredient

Magnesium (often magnesium glycinate)

What it targets: Magnesium is involved in the calming, inhibitory side of nervous-system signaling and in muscle relaxation. The glycinate form is gentle on digestion and pairs magnesium with glycine, which is useful here.

Commonly used amount: Around 200 to 400 mg of elemental magnesium in the evening. Higher amounts can loosen stools, so many people start low.

Best for: A busy or tense mind, muscle restlessness, and lighter sleep.

Glycine

What it targets: Glycine is an amino acid associated with a gentle drop in core body temperature through improved circulation to the skin. That temperature dip is part of how the body settles into sleep, which makes glycine a logical match for temperature-driven awakenings.

Commonly used amount: Around 3 g taken shortly before bed.

Best for: Warm nights, night sweats, and difficulty settling in a warm room.

L-theanine

What it targets: An amino acid found in tea, L-theanine is associated with a calm, focused state without heavy sedation. It may support winding down when the obstacle is mental rather than physical.

Commonly used amount: Around 100 to 200 mg in the evening.

Best for: Anxious, looping thoughts at lights-out.

Reishi

What it targets: Reishi is a mushroom used traditionally as a calming adaptogen. It is reached for to support an overall sense of relaxation rather than to force sleep. Evidence in humans is still developing, so consider it a gentle addition, not a centerpiece.

Best for: People who want a low-key calming option alongside the amino acids above.

Timing and How to Use Them

  • Take calming ingredients (magnesium, L-theanine, glycine) roughly 30 to 60 minutes before bed so they are active as you settle.
  • Change one variable at a time. Give a single ingredient one to two weeks before adding another, so you can tell what is actually helping.
  • Support the pathway with behavior. A cool bedroom around 65 to 68 degrees works alongside glycine on the temperature side; a consistent wake time supports your overall sleep drive.
  • Keep amounts modest. More is not better with sleep supplements, and gentle, consistent use tends to be easier to sustain.

Who Should Be Cautious

General education is not personal advice, and perimenopause often overlaps with other health changes. Speak with your clinician before starting if any of the following apply:

  • You have kidney disease, which affects how the body handles magnesium.
  • You are pregnant or breastfeeding.
  • You take hormone therapy, an antidepressant, a sedative, or a blood thinner, since interactions are possible and reishi in particular is worth flagging if you are on blood thinners.
  • You have low blood pressure or are managing a bleeding disorder.

It is also worth raising the bigger picture with a clinician: whether hormone therapy fits your situation, whether thyroid function or iron levels are contributing, and whether any current medication could be part of the sleep change. Supplements can be one supporting layer, but they work best when the underlying drivers are understood.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does melatonin help perimenopausal sleep?

Melatonin is a timing signal, so it is most useful when your body clock is shifted, for example with jet lag or a late schedule. If your main problem is warm, anxious middle-of-the-night waking, a timing aid is often a poor match for the symptom. Many people in perimenopause find the calming and thermoregulatory ingredients above more relevant.

Can I combine these with hormone therapy?

Possibly, but this is exactly the kind of question to bring to the clinician who prescribed it, so your full picture is considered together.

How long before I know if something is working?

Give a single ingredient about one to two weeks of consistent nightly use before judging it. If nothing shifts, change the ingredient rather than stacking more on top.

The bottom line: Perimenopausal sleep responds best when you match the ingredient to the symptom. Target calm with L-theanine and magnesium glycinate, target temperature with glycine and a cool room, and bring the larger hormonal picture to your clinician.
